The Life and Work of Marie Curie

Marie Curie is probably the most famous woman scientist who has ever lived. Born Maria Sklodowska in Poland in 1867, she is famous for her work on radioactivity, and was twice a winner of the Nobel Prize. With her husband, Pierre Curie, and Henri Becquerel, she was awarded the 1903 Nobel Prize for Physics, and was then sole winner of the 1911 Nobel Prize for Chemistry. She was the first woman to win a Nobel Prize.

From childhood, Marie was remarkable for her prodigious memory, and at the age of 16 won a gold medal on completion of her secondary education. Because her father lost his savings through bad investment, she then had to take work as a teacher. From her earnings she was able to finance her sister Bronia's medical studies in Paris, on the understanding that Bronia would, in turn, later help her to get an education.

In 1891 this promise was fulfilled and Marie went to Paris and began to study at the Sorbonne (the University of Paris). She often worked far into the night and lived on little more than bread and butter and tea. She came first in the examination in the physical sciences in 1893, and in 1894 was placed second in the examination in mathematical sciences. It was not until the spring of that year that she was introduced to Pierre Curie.

Their marriage in 1895 marked the start of a partnership that was soon to achieve results of world significance. Following Henri Becquerel's discovery in 1896 of a new phenomenon, which Marie later called 'radioactivity', Marie Curie decided to find out if the radioactivity discovered in uranium was to be found in other elements. She discovered that this was true for thorium.

Turning her attention to minerals, she found her interest drawn to pitchblende, a mineral whose radioactivity, superior to that of pure uranium, could be explained only by the presence in the ore of small quantities of an unknown substance of very high activity. Pierre Curie joined her in the work that she had undertaken to resolve this problem, and that led to the discovery of the new elements, polonium and radium.

On 19 April 1906, Pierre Curie was killed in a street accident. Marie was left to raise her two young daughters and to continue their scientific work. On May 13, 1906, she was appointed to the professorship that had been left vacant on her husband's death, becoming the first woman to teach at the Sorbonne. In 1911 she was awarded the Nobel Prize for Chemistry for isolating radium.

The Development of the Modern Computer

A. The history of the modern computer is a fascinating journey through centuries of mathematical innovation, engineering triumphs, and technological breakthroughs. Long before electronic computers were developed, humans relied on mechanical devices to assist with calculation. The abacus, used in ancient civilizations, was one of the earliest tools created for arithmetic operations.

B. In the 19th century, Charles Babbage, an English mathematician and mechanical engineer, originated the concept of a programmable computer. Considered the "father of the computer", he conceptualized and invented the first mechanical computer, the Difference Engine, in the early 1820s. Later, he proposed the Analytical Engine, a device that contained an arithmetic logic unit, control flow in the form of conditional branching and loops, and integrated memory.

C. During the first half of the 20th century, many scientific computing needs were met by increasingly sophisticated analog computers. However, these were non-programmable and lacked the versatility of modern digital devices. The transition from analog to digital computing began in the late 1930s. John Atanasoff and Clifford Berry designed the Atanasoff-Berry Computer (ABC) in 1937, which was the first electronic digital computing device.

D. The ENIAC (Electronic Numerical Integrator and Computer) was built during World War II in the United States and completed in 1945. It was the first programmable, electronic, general-purpose digital computer. ENIAC was enormous, weighing over 27 tons and taking up 1,800 square feet. It was capable of calculating complex trajectories for artillery shells, demonstrating the incredible power of electronic computation.

E. The invention of the transistor in 1947 by Bell Labs revolutionized computing. Transistors replaced vacuum tubes, allowing computers to become smaller, faster, more reliable, and more energy-efficient. By the 1970s, the development of microprocessors allowed entire central processing units (CPUs) to fit on a single microchip, laying the groundwork for the personal computer (PC) revolution.

Artificial Intelligence and the Future of Work

Artificial Intelligence (AI) has rapidly transitioned from theoretical science fiction to a dominant force driving modern global industry. Powered by advanced machine learning algorithms and massive computing infrastructure, AI systems are now capable of performing complex analytical tasks, pattern recognition, and creative generation that were once thought to be exclusive domain of human intelligence.

Economists and technology analysts hold diverse opinions regarding the impact of automation on the global workforce. Optimists argue that AI will primarily serve as a powerful tool to augment human capabilities rather than replace them outright. According to this perspective, routine and repetitive manual tasks will be automated, freeing human workers to concentrate on higher-level strategic thinking, creative problem-solving, and interpersonal communication.

Conversely, skeptics warn that the pace of current technological transformation differs fundamentally from historical industrial revolutions. Previous technological shifts primarily automated physical labor while creating new service sector positions. In contrast, modern generative AI and neural networks directly target cognitive and knowledge-based professions, potentially leading to widespread displacement across legal, administrative, software development, and analytical fields.

To successfully navigate this transitional era, educational institutions and corporate training programs must shift focus toward lifelong learning and continuous skill development. Emphasis must be placed on developing uniquely human attributes—such as emotional intelligence, critical thinking, ethical judgment, and complex cross-disciplinary problem solving—that remain difficult for algorithmic architectures to replicate.
